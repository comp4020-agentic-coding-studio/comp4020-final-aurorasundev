# What should we test, and what should we judge for ourselves?

I let Claude write a lot of Throwaway's code, but two of the changes that mattered most came from trying the app myself. The Gallery could play music, yet the records sounded so similar that I struggled to tell them apart. A return key gave me back the right to burn a paper I had kept, but I still had to find the paper by chance. I changed the Gallery so a note's anger could become something calm, and I replaced the return key with an optional account. **My position is that developers working with coding agents should turn clearly defined promises into repeatable checks and use the product themselves to decide which promises matter and whether the experience lives up to them.**

Barke, James and Polikarpova observed twenty programmers using GitHub Copilot. Sometimes they knew what to do next and used the tool to move faster. Sometimes they used it to explore their options. The study observed inline code completion, so it cannot establish how much today's agents can do on their own. I find the distinction useful for deciding when to write a test. I can ask Claude to compare several designs while I am still working out what the interaction should do. Once I decide the fire must wait for the server to confirm the burn, I can ask the agent to implement that sequence and add a regression check. A test written before the decision could tie the team to an interaction it has barely tried. [Barke et al., *Grounded Copilot*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15000)

Where Barke and colleagues help me decide when to settle a requirement, Anthropic's guide helps me choose how to evaluate it. It distinguishes code checks, model grading and human judgement, and says model grades need calibration against human judgement. Choosing the right grader still leaves the question of what to grade. In Throwaway, an early test could show that at most 48 records were visible while visitors had no way to reach the 49th. Reaching later records is testable once someone notices what the visitor needs to do. With music, a test can check allowed tempos and timbres. I still need to compare samples to judge whether the image and sound together convey the intended change in feeling. I would use a model to screen those samples where its ratings can be checked against human examples, with people still spot-checking its decisions. That leaves more time for cases where the criteria are unclear or getting the decision wrong would be costly. [Anthropic, *Demystifying evals for AI agents*](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)

Bainbridge gives me another reason to put human review early. Writing about industrial automation, she describes operators who must take over when something goes wrong, even though routine monitoring gives them little practice at doing so. The stakes in a software project are different, but a developer who sees only the agent's summaries and passing checks can also be left to reconstruct the system when it fails. A team could have another developer try a flow as two different users and explain when it saves, when it refuses an action and how it recovers. Doing this while the design can still change gives the team practice understanding the system as well as a chance to spot mistakes. [Bainbridge, *Ironies of Automation*](https://tc.ifac-control.org/4/1/newsletter/ironies-of-automation/view)

Does all that review still save time? In METR's early 2025 randomised trial, sixteen experienced open source developers worked on tasks in repositories they knew. Tasks where AI was allowed took 19 percent longer. In 2026, METR said newer tools might be speeding developers up, but participant and task selection made the size of that effect hard to estimate. Those findings cannot tell me how much time I saved on Throwaway. They give me a reason to measure the time from starting a task to accepting it, including waiting, review and rework. A regression test can pay for itself when permissions change repeatedly. A quick prototype that people can try can be a better use of review time while the design is still unsettled. [METR, 2025 trial](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) · [METR, 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

In a booking system, for example, I would use a concurrency test to check that two people cannot book the same slot. To learn whether a first-time user mistakes a waitlist entry for a confirmed booking, I would watch them try the flow. If several people misread the same message, I would change the interface, try it again with a new user and turn any clear rule I discovered into a check. When the team changes models or developers, the check and the reason for it are still there. The developer's job is to decide what evidence each promise needs and understand what that evidence actually shows.

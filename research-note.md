# What should we test, and what should we judge for ourselves?

I let Claude write a lot of Throwaway's code, but two of the changes that mattered most came from trying the app myself. The Gallery could play music, yet the records sounded so similar that I struggled to tell them apart. A return key gave me back the right to burn a paper I had kept, but I still had to find the paper by chance. I changed the Gallery so a note's anger could become something calm, and I replaced the return key with an optional account. **When I work with a coding agent, trying the product myself helps me see what is worth checking. Once a promise is clear, I turn it into a repeatable test. For changes in how the product feels, feedback from people who didn't build it helps me judge the result.**

Barke, James and Polikarpova observed twenty programmers using GitHub Copilot. They used Copilot to move faster when they knew what to do next and to explore when they were unsure. I use their distinction, drawn from watching inline code completion, to decide when to write a test in a longer agent workflow. While I am still working out what an interaction should do, I can ask Claude to compare several designs. Once I decide the fire must wait for the server to confirm the burn, the sequence can be covered by a regression check. A test written before the decision could tie the team to an interaction it has barely tried. [Barke et al., *Grounded Copilot*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15000)

Where Barke and colleagues help me decide when to settle a requirement, Anthropic's guide helps me choose how to evaluate it. It distinguishes code checks, model grading and human judgement, and says model grades need calibration against human judgement. Even a well chosen grader can answer the wrong question. In Throwaway, for instance, an early test could show that at most 48 records were visible, yet visitors still had no way to reach the 49th. Once that gap was clear, a test could follow a visitor through to the later records. For the music, allowed tempos and timbres can be checked automatically, but people need to look and listen to judge whether the image and sound carry the intended change in feeling. I would use a model to screen samples after checking its ratings against listeners' judgements, and have people spot-check its decisions. That leaves more time for cases where the criteria are unclear or getting the decision wrong would be costly. [Anthropic, *Demystifying evals for AI agents*](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)

In Bainbridge's account of industrial automation, operators are asked to take over when something goes wrong, even though routine monitoring gives them little practice at it. If all I see of a feature is the agent's summary and green checks, the first failure may also be the first time I work through it. I'd ask a second developer to try a flow in two sessions, disconnect one and work out which actions were saved and what should happen on reconnect. That way, we can practise tracing a failure while we can still change the design. [Bainbridge, *Ironies of Automation*](https://tc.ifac-control.org/4/1/newsletter/ironies-of-automation/view)

After METR's 2025 trial, the developers estimated that AI had saved them time, even though tasks where AI was allowed took longer on average. If developers can misjudge their own speed, how sure am I that the new Gallery records sound less alike? Knowing what I changed can colour what I hear, even when I spot-check a model's ratings. My own listening can flag a problem, so I use it to decide what to revisit. To judge the revision, I'd ask people outside the project to compare old and new recordings made from the same sample briefs, without telling them which version they are hearing. Their descriptions would tell me which tracks sound distinct and where they hear the mood shift. In the walkthrough, the second developer can also describe what they expected and what happened before I explain the design. If we disagree, I have something specific to investigate. [METR, 2025 trial](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)

In a booking system, a concurrency test can check that two people cannot book the same slot, while watching a first-time user try the flow can reveal whether they mistake a waitlist entry for a confirmed booking. If several people misread the same message, I would change the interface, try it again with a new user and turn any clear rule I discovered into a check. I would use repeatable tests to check that the system keeps its promises and judge the experience by watching people use it and asking what they thought had happened.
